Things to try

  1. One layer = one straight line. With Hidden layers: None the network computes a weighted sum of the two features, so its cat/dog boundary is always a straight line, which is the perceptron idea. Train on Easy.
  2. Where a line fails. Switch to Ring and train with no hidden layer. Accuracy gets stuck. Now pick 4 hidden neurons. Each dashed line is one hidden neuron, and the output combines them into a curved boundary.
  3. Gradient descent, one step at a time. Hover a point, then press Step batch repeatedly. Watch the loss, the gradients and the weight nudges in Under the hood.
  4. Learning rate. Try 0.003, which is painfully slow, then 3, which is jumpy and can blow up.
  5. Overfitting. Use Overlapping, 20 examples, 32-16 hidden and Show test points. Training accuracy keeps climbing while test accuracy doesn't.
  6. Click the plot to add your own cats and dogs.
  1. Start with AND. Press Next example a few times. The perceptron looks at one example at a time and moves its line only when it gets one wrong.
  2. Try OR and NAND. It always gets there. Whenever some straight line can separate the 1s from the 0s, the perceptron learning rule is guaranteed to find one. This is the perceptron convergence theorem.
  3. Now XOR. Press Run. The line keeps swinging back and forth and the mistakes never reach 0, because no straight line can do this job.
  4. A famous limitation. This weakness of single-layer perceptrons was analysed in Minsky and Papert's 1969 book Perceptrons, and it dampened enthusiasm for neural networks for years.
  5. The fix is at the bottom of the page: a hidden layer. Compare the hand-built solution with one found by backpropagation.
  6. Make your own problems by clicking the dots. Of the 16 possible two-input problems, only XOR and its opposite (XNOR) are impossible for one perceptron.
  1. A map of every possible model. This model has just two knobs, w and b, so we can draw its loss for every setting. Real networks have millions of knobs, but the idea is the same.
  2. Take 1 step. The arrow points straight downhill: the negative gradient. Each step is learning rate × slope, so steps shrink as the ground flattens near the bottom.
  3. Learning rate. At 0.1 it crawls. Around 3 it's quick and smooth. Push it toward 50 and it overshoots, jumping across the valley.
  4. Stochastic gradient descent. Measure the gradient on 1 random animal. Each step is cheaper but noisier, so the path wobbles, yet it still heads downhill on average and never quite settles.
  5. Momentum. The ball keeps some speed from step to step, so it races along the long, gently sloping valley floor. It may also overshoot and circle back.
  6. Watch the right-hand plot. As the point walks downhill, the S-curve gets better at separating cats from dogs.
  1. Step through one round with Next ▶. The forward pass makes a prediction, the loss scores it, and the backward pass sends the blame back through the network, one layer at a time.
  2. Spot the pattern. Every weight's gradient is (error signal δ of the neuron it feeds) × (the value that came in on its wire). Backprop computes each δ once and reuses it for every weight feeding that neuron.
  3. Check the "nudge" column. Nudging each weight and re-measuring the loss gives the same numbers. Backprop gets all of them in one backward sweep instead of one extra forward pass per weight, which matters when there are billions of weights.
  4. Right answers barely teach. Train 20 rounds, then step through again. Once p is close to y, p − y is tiny and so is every gradient.
  5. Vanishing gradients. Press Random weights until a hidden neuron sits near 0 or 1. Its slope h(1−h) is almost 0, so almost no blame reaches the weights behind it.
  1. No hidden layer = template matching. Set Hidden layers: None and train. The two weight images become a "cat template" and a "dog template". A picture is scored by how much it overlaps each one.
  2. Hidden neurons learn features. With 16 hidden neurons, look at the weight images. Some respond to pointy ears or whiskers and others to floppy side ears or a muzzle. Nobody programmed those in.
  3. Draw your own in the inspector. Triangles on top suggest a cat, and long ears hanging at the sides suggest a dog. Erase parts of a real picture to find which pixels matter.
  4. Overfitting. Use 50 training images, 60% noise and 32-16 hidden. Training accuracy reaches 100% while test accuracy lags behind.
  5. Click any test picture below to send it through the network.
